# Contradictions — Patient A

Eight, across three documents. None implies an error by anyone. Each changes what
gets tested next.

---

## 1. A reflex present at M62, absent at M63

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| M62, H-reflex study | **Present and normal bilaterally** — 31.0 ms, 31.1 ms |
| M63, examination | **"Ankle jerks are absent"** |

The H-reflex is the ankle jerk tested electrically rather than with a hammer. At
M62 the circuit worked. At M63 it could not be elicited.

**Either** genuinely lost in four weeks — evidence of fast progression, and a
reason to escalate — **or** hard to test in a patient who could no longer be
positioned normally, since ankle jerks depend heavily on position and relaxation.

Ask for a careful re-check. Which it is changes how fast everything else must move.

*Kind 1 — same thing measured twice, different answers.*

## 2. Position sense "preserved" at the bedside, damaged on the machine

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| M62, SSEP | Tibial responses **prolonged** — posterior columns conducting slowly |
| M63, examination | **"Joint position sense is preserved"** |

Likely resolution: bedside testing is crude and the more sensitive test was not
done. **Vibration sense is recorded nowhere** — and in B12 deficiency vibration goes
*first*, well before position sense.

A tuning fork at the toes takes under a minute and would settle it.

*Kind 2 — machine disagrees with bedside.*

---

## 3. "No large-fibre neuropathy" vs burning feet — a correction to the family's own earlier reading

An earlier family write-up read the M62 study as excluding chemotherapy nerve
damage. **Too broad.**

Nerve conduction studies test **large** fibres only. They say nothing about **small**
fibres — pain and temperature. Burning in a stocking distribution with a normal
study is the classic picture of small-fibre neuropathy, and chemotherapy is among
its commonest causes.

So the chemotherapy probably *did* leave a mark, and it likely explains the burning.
It does **not** explain the weakness, the bladder, or the cord findings.

*Run this pass over your own previous conclusions, not only the clinicians'.*

## 4. F-wave persistence reduced in the data, latency reported as normal

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Data, peroneal nerve | Persistence **4/10 right, 6/10 left** |
| Summary | *"F-wave latencies are normal bilaterally"* |

The summary is accurate and is **not a statement about persistence**. Reduced
peroneal F-wave persistence can be a normal variant — but it sits alongside burning
feet and absent ankle jerks.

*Kind 3 — a summary that is accurate but narrower than how it will be read. This is
the most common and most under-appreciated kind.*

---

## 5. Conduction velocities at the boundary, summarised as normal

Tibial motor conduction velocities **40.6 and 41.9 m/s** — at or just below the usual
lower limit of normal, summarised as *"normal conduction velocities."*

Not necessarily wrong. Reference ranges vary by laboratory. Worth a second look
given the peripheral findings that appeared a month later.

*Kind 3 again.*

---

## 6. Two different nerves named in one report

The summary lists *"lateral **femoral** cutaneous nerves"* among those tested. The
data pages record *"lateral **antebrachial** cutaneous nerve"* — a forearm nerve, not
a thigh nerve.

One is a slip. **Which was actually studied?** It matters: one says something about
the leg, the other does not.

*Kind 4 — internal inconsistency. Clerical, common, and it propagates.*

---

## 7. The study was framed around a cause before it was run

The M62 referral abstract reads: *"...bilateral lower limb weakness **post-radiation
therapy**. To assess for lower limb weakness."*

Radiation named as context before any testing. Nothing in the imaging supports
radiation myelopathy. Radiation is the easy answer that closes investigations.

Ask what evidence supports it, rather than arguing against it. Asking for the
evidence is what reopens an investigation.

*Not a contradiction between findings — between a question asked and a question
answered.*

---

## 8. A fact in the notes, blank on the prescription

The sulfa allergy appears in the M63 clinical notes. The **"Any Allergies" field on
the M63 prescription is empty.**

The patient has a long-term urinary catheter. Catheter-associated infection is very
often treated with a sulfonamide antibiotic. In a busy emergency department with a
febrile catheterised patient, that is the reflex prescription — and the prescription
sheet is what someone reaches for.

*Kind 5 — the record disagrees with what is written where it will actually be read.
Not a diagnostic finding. Possibly the most consequential item on this page.*
