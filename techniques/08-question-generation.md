# 8 — Question generation

**Turn findings into questions a clinician will engage with.**

## What it is

The conversion step. Everything the previous seven techniques produced becomes a
short, ordered list of questions you can hand over — and the form of the question
decides whether it works.

## Why form matters this much

You get fifteen or twenty minutes. In that time a clinician will either think with
you about this case, or defend decisions already made. **Which of those happens is
mostly determined by how you phrase the first question.**

This is not about being deferential. It is that a defensive answer contains no
information, and you came for information.

## The rules

**Ask, never assert.** *"Does that combination point to X?"* not *"this is X."* You
may be right and it does not matter — an assertion from a family member invites
correction, and correction is the end of the useful part of the conversation.

**Quote their own record back, neutrally.** *"Your note says the retention has not
been formally evaluated — can we arrange that?"* is unanswerable and carries no
accusation. Nothing you could phrase yourself is as effective as their own
sentence.

**Name the diagnosis you want excluded.** *"Can we exclude a dural AV fistula?"*
gets different imaging than *"can we have a scan?"* Naming it tells the radiologist
what sequences to run, and it signals that a specific question is being asked.

**Lead with the one-sentence version of the whole case.** The strongest question is
usually the one that states the pattern in language the specialty uses, and asks
what it means. It demonstrates in ten seconds that the file has been read.

**Ask for a plan, not a promise.** *"What is our plan if she keeps declining at this
rate?"* gets an answer. *"Will she be paralysed?"* gets a hedge, and makes everything
after it vaguer. Nobody can promise an outcome; everybody can describe a plan.

**Put the action items first and make them not-questions.** Some outputs are not
questions at all — draw the blood before the tablet, call about contrast. Separate
them at the top under "before anything else", so they do not compete with the
questions for the clinician's attention.

**Ten questions is too many. Three is right.** Order them so that if you only get
through three, they were the three that mattered. Keep the rest on the page under
"also worth raising" — sometimes there is time.

## Structure of the file

1. **Before anything else** — actions, not questions
2. **The questions, in priority order** — each with one line underneath saying why
   it is phrased that way, for whoever uses the list
3. **Also worth raising** — the overflow
4. **Emergency** — what sends you to hospital rather than an appointment
5. **Status unknown** — a checklist of what you need to fill in before this list is
   usable again

That last section matters more than it looks. A question list goes stale the moment
anything happens, and a stale list used confidently is worse than no list.

## What it produced in the worked example

Eight questions. The first one stated the entire case in a single sentence using
the term the specialty uses, and asked whether it pointed where it appears to. The
second raised the sequencing problem from
[technique 7](07-iatrogenic-check.md) as a question about test reliability rather
than as a correction. The third named a specific curable condition to be excluded
and asked whether the scan would use contrast.

Two items sat above the questions as actions, because they needed doing that week
regardless of any appointment.

The last question — *"she went from a walker to a wheelchair in four weeks; what is
our plan if she keeps declining at this rate?"* — was the most important one on the
page, and it is the one that would have been left out of an unprepared conversation
because it is the hardest to ask.
