# 5 — Gap analysis

**What was ordered, against what this pattern actually calls for.**

## What it is

Two tables. What has been ordered or done, with a verdict on each. And what has
not been ordered, with a reason why it matters.

## When it pays

After every appointment that generates orders, which is most of them.

The structural reason this works: a specialist orders tests from **their** branch
of the differential. A rheumatologist's panel looks rheumatological; a
spine surgeon's workup looks surgical. Each is reasonable within its frame. Nobody
is positioned to notice that the pattern in front of them points somewhere else
entirely — because nobody else's frame is in the room.

You have all the frames, because you have been to all the appointments.

## How to run it

**Table one — ordered.** Every test, with a one-line verdict: right test, reasonable
to exclude, or already answered by something you have. Mark the ones that are
exactly right. This matters — the output of this technique should not read as a
list of complaints, and usually the important tests *are* on the list.

**Table two — missing.** For each: what it is, and **why it matters for this
specific pattern**. "Would be nice to have" is not a reason. "Confirms or excludes
the leading candidate when the primary test is unreliable" is.

Group the missing items by what they cost:

- **Same needle, same visit** — blood tests that can be added to a draw already
  happening. These are nearly free and the ask is trivial.
- **Separate but independent** — things that do not wait on the diagnosis at all.
  Kidney function after months of catheterisation. A bone scan after years of
  immobility.
- **Bedside and free** — a tuning fork, a lying-and-standing blood pressure. Under
  five minutes, no equipment, frequently decisive.

**Table three — care gaps.** Not diagnostic at all. The things that arrive with a
change in condition and appear in nobody's notes because they are nobody's
specialty. Pressure sores when someone starts using a wheelchair. Clot risk with
new immobility. Whether physiotherapy should continue and in what form.

This third table is where family-held knowledge is most valuable, because it is the
only table where you know things the clinicians structurally cannot.

## The tone rule

Write every entry as something to **ask for**, never as something that was missed.
This is not diplomacy for its own sake — "why wasn't this ordered?" produces a
defence, and a defence closes the conversation. "Could we add these to the same
draw?" produces a signature.

Same information. Completely different outcome.

## What it caught in the worked example

The ordered panel leaned rheumatological and inflammatory — reasonable, and the two
single most important tests *were* on it. But roughly ten investigations that
speak directly to the leading candidate were absent, and most could have been added
to a blood draw that was already scheduled.

The highest-value items were not diagnostic at all:

- kidney function after three months of catheterisation, because backed-up urine
  damages kidneys silently and this does not wait for a neurological answer
- a bedside vibration test — under a minute, no equipment — that would have resolved
  a direct contradiction between two reports
- a lying-and-standing blood pressure, free, addressing the one symptom in the
  entire file that nothing explained

None of these required knowing more medicine than the documents already contained.
They required somebody holding all the documents at once.
