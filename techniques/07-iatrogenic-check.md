# 7 — Iatrogenic check

**Does a treatment invalidate a pending test, or worsen a documented symptom?**

*This is the technique that earns the repo.*

## What it is

Take everything newly prescribed. Take everything newly ordered. Take the symptom
list. Cross them against each other, in three directions:

1. **Treatment × pending test** — will taking this make that test unreadable?
2. **Treatment × existing symptom** — does this medicine cause something she already has?
3. **Treatment × treatment** — do two of these stack an effect nobody intended?

## When it pays

**Every single time a prescription and a test order come out of the same
consultation**, which is most consultations.

## Why this gap exists

Nothing here is a mistake by anybody. The prescription and the test order are
written in the same two minutes, at the end of a long appointment, by someone
holding the diagnosis in their head rather than the logistics. The lab receives a
request form and no information about medication. The pharmacist dispenses a brand
name and sees no test orders. The patient is told to start the tablets and told to
get the blood done, and nobody says which comes first **because at no point does
one person hold both pieces of paper**.

You hold both pieces of paper. That is the entire basis of this technique.

## How to run it

### Direction 1 — treatment × pending test

For each new medicine, find its **active ingredients** — not its brand name.
Then for each pending test, ask: is this medicine, or anything in it, the thing
being measured? Or does it change the thing being measured?

Supplements are the dangerous case, because the brand name conceals the molecule
and because "it's just a vitamin" switches off suspicion. A vitamin supplement
started before a vitamin level is drawn makes that level meaningless — and it fails
in the worst possible direction: the result comes back **normal**, which looks like
an answer and closes the branch.

If the sequencing has already gone wrong: **do not stop anything unilaterally.**
Note the exact start date, tell the doctor and the lab, and ask whether there are
downstream markers that stay abnormal longer and can still reveal what the
supplement has masked. There usually are.

### Direction 2 — treatment × existing symptom

List the documented symptoms. For each new medicine, read its common adverse
effects against that list. You are looking for a medicine whose side effect is
something the patient **already has** — because that effect will be invisible as a
side effect. It will be read as the disease progressing.

### Direction 3 — treatment × treatment

Two medicines with the same side effect do not add that effect twice; they add it
more than twice in practice. Worth asking whether they can be introduced one at a
time, especially when the shared effect is the thing that caused the admission.

Also: **two supplements can contain the same ingredient.** Check every component of
every product against every other. Confirm from the physical packs, with the
pharmacist — never from the brand name, and never from a model.

## The three rules

**Find the molecule, not the brand.** Brand names vary by country, change
formulation without changing name, and often contain more than the name suggests.

**Never advise stopping anything.** The output of this technique is always a
question for the prescriber. Always. A family member stopping a medicine on the
basis of an internet reading is a worse outcome than the problem being checked.

**Raise it as a question, not a catch.** *"She started X on the day the Y test was
ordered — will that make the result unreliable?"* Most clinicians see it instantly
and fix it. The same information delivered as a correction produces a defence.

## What it caught in the worked example

**Direction 1.** A supplement and a blood test, from the same consultation, on the
same day. The supplement's principal active ingredient **was the substance the
blood test measures.** Taken first, the test would have read normal regardless of
the truth — and the candidate it was ordered to investigate was the most treatable
one on the list, and the only one that was cheap and reversible.

That single finding was worth more than every other output of the method combined.
It cost nothing to act on: draw the blood first.

**Direction 2.** Two of five new medicines list adverse effects that are **precisely
the two problems the patient already had** — urinary retention and dizziness
causing falls. Both would present as the disease worsening. Neither would be
attributed to the tablet.

**Direction 3.** Two of the prescribed supplements plausibly contain the same B
vitamin, one of which damages nerves at high doses over long periods — in a patient
being treated for nerve damage, for three months. Not dangerous as prescribed;
worth confirming from the packs.

## The general shape

The highest-value findings in this method are not medical insights. They are
**sequencing and logistics problems that are invisible from inside a single
appointment and obvious from outside it.**

You do not need to know more medicine than the clinicians. You need to be the
person holding both pieces of paper.
