# 6 — Contradiction pass

**Read every report against every other. Disagreements are high-yield.**

## What it is

A deliberate hunt for places where two documents disagree — or where one document
disagrees with itself.

## When it pays

Whenever you have two or more documents covering the same ground, and especially
when they come from different departments or are separated in time.

Why this is productive: each report is written by someone who read the referral
and perhaps one prior result. **Nobody reads all of them side by side.** The
contradictions are genuinely invisible from inside the system, and they are sitting
there in plain text.

## The five kinds worth hunting

**1. Same thing measured twice, different answers.** Usually separated in time.
Either something genuinely changed — which tells you the rate of progression — or
one measurement was unreliable. Both are worth knowing and the distinction is
often urgent.

**2. Machine disagrees with bedside.** An instrument found something a physical
examination did not, or the reverse. Usually the resolution is that one method is
more sensitive, and the more sensitive test was not the one that was done. This
reliably generates a cheap, specific request.

**3. A report's summary disagrees with its own data.** Read the numbers, then read
the conclusion, and check that the conclusion is about the same thing. Summaries
compress, and compression drops qualifiers. "F-wave *latencies* are normal" is a
precise statement that does not cover persistence — but it reads as "F-waves are
normal" to everyone downstream, forever.

**4. Internal inconsistency.** The summary names one thing, the data table names
another. Transposed values. A field that contradicts another field on the same
page. These are clerical, they are common, and they propagate.

**5. The record disagrees with what is written where it will be read.** A fact is
in the notes but absent from the document someone would actually pick up in an
emergency. Not a contradiction between findings — a contradiction between what is
known and where it lives.

## How to run it

Go **claim by claim**, not document by document. For each factual claim in each
document, ask: does anything else in the file address this? If yes, do they agree?

It is slow. It is the single highest-yield pass in the method, and it is the one
worth doing with a model — holding six documents in mind at once is exactly what
they are good at. Verify every hit against the paper yourself.

## How to write them up

For each: the two sources, what each says, the two possible resolutions, and what
would settle it. **Then stop.** Do not decide which is right.

And never write it up as an error. Every one of these has an innocent explanation,
most of them *are* innocent, and the one you are most confident about is the one
most likely to be your own misreading.

## What it caught in the worked example

Eight, across three documents:

- A reflex **present and normal** on an electrical test, **absent** on examination
  four weeks later. Either genuinely lost in four weeks — which changes the
  urgency of everything — or difficult to elicit in a patient who could no longer
  be positioned normally. Nobody had noticed, because the two findings are in two
  different documents written by two different people.
- A pathway reported as **damaged** by an instrument and **preserved** by bedside
  testing — resolved by the observation that the more sensitive bedside test was
  never recorded at all.
- A summary line stating that certain latencies were normal, where the underlying
  data showed a **different parameter** of the same measurement was reduced. The
  summary was accurate. It was also narrower than how it would be read.
- A nerve named one way in the conclusions and **another way in the data tables**.
- A fact recorded in the clinical notes and **left blank on the prescription**,
  where it was the one piece of information that mattered in an emergency.

Also caught: a correction to the family's **own earlier analysis**, which had read
a normal nerve conduction study as excluding a cause it could not actually speak
to. Run this pass over your own previous conclusions, not just the clinicians'.
